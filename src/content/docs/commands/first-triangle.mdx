Every piece exists: a swapchain with image views, a pipeline, a command buffer. This lesson puts them in motion and
draws one triangle, frame after frame. The new question is coordination: the CPU, the GPU and the presentation engine
work at their own pace, so how does each one know when it may touch an image?

## Where this fits

The last lesson of the commands module and the end of the first big arc of the course: from `vkCreateInstance` to
pixels on the screen. It uses everything from modules 4 to 6, and it is the first program in which the GPU actually
executes work. One frame is this chain:

`wait fence → acquire image → record (barrier, rendering, draw, barrier) → submit → present`

The [frame loop](term:frame-loop) is built on the [swapchain](lesson:swapchain) (acquire and present), the
[pipeline](lesson:graphics-pipeline), [dynamic rendering](lesson:dynamic-rendering) and
[command buffers](lesson:command-buffers), held together by a fence and semaphores. Module 7 (synchronization) starts
from this loop and makes it fast: several frames in flight, timeline semaphores, and recreating the swapchain.

## The frame loop

Three parties take part in every frame, each running on its own:

- the **CPU** records commands and submits them;
- the **GPU queue** executes them, later;
- the **presentation engine** shows images and releases them when they leave the screen.

Nobody may use an image or a command buffer while another party still does. Vulkan gives a separate tool for each kind
of "wait until":

- a [fence](term:vk-fence) - **GPU → CPU**: a submission signals it when its work completes, and the CPU waits on it;
- a [semaphore](term:vk-semaphore) - **GPU → GPU**: something on the device side signals it and a queue operation waits
  on it, and the CPU is not involved. In our frame the presentation engine signals `image_available` when an acquired
  image is really free (the acquire itself is a host call, not a queue operation), the batch waits on it and signals
  `render_finished`, and the present waits on that and signals nothing;
- a [pipeline barrier](term:pipeline-barrier) - **inside a command buffer**: orders commands before it against
  commands after it, and can change an image's layout on the way.

These form a hierarchy from coarse to fine: the fence orders whole submissions against the CPU, the semaphore orders
GPU-side operations against each other, the barrier orders individual commands. (The [queue-sync](lab:queue-sync)
interactive lets you play with the first two.) One frame then goes like this:

1. **wait** for the fence of the previous frame: its command buffer is free again;
2. **acquire** a swapchain image; the presentation engine will signal `image_available` when it is really ours;
3. **record**: barrier to the attachment layout, rendering, draw, barrier to the present layout;
4. **submit**: wait on `image_available`, run, signal `render_finished` and the fence;
5. **present**: the presentation engine waits on `render_finished`, then shows the image.

## 1. The sync objects

<Step file="09_triangle" region="sync-objects" />

One fence, one `image_available` semaphore, and one `render_finished` semaphore **per swapchain image** - three here.
Why per image? `vkQueuePresentKHR` waits on `render_finished`, but we cannot know when the presentation engine has
finished that wait. It is only certain once the *same image* comes back from a later acquire. Indexing the semaphore by
the image index ties its reuse to exactly that moment. A single shared semaphore could be signaled again while the
previous present still waits on it.

The fence is created **signaled**, because the very first frame waits on it before anything was ever submitted;
an unsignaled fence would make it wait forever.

## 2. Wait for the previous frame

<Step file="09_triangle" region="wait-fence" />

With one frame in flight the CPU waits here until the GPU has finished the previous frame. Only then may the command
buffer be reset and re-recorded (a *pending* buffer must not be touched) and `image_available` reused. The CPU and
GPU take turns instead of overlapping; making them overlap is the first lesson of module 7.

The fence is *not* reset here, only after a successful acquire. If the acquire failed and we returned early with a reset
fence, the next frame would wait on a fence that nothing will ever signal.

## 3. Acquire an image

<Step file="09_triangle" region="acquire" />

`vkAcquireNextImageKHR` returns an image **index** right away, but the image may still be on the screen or waiting for
its vblank. The semaphore is the real answer: it is signaled when the presentation engine has let go of the image. It
must be unsignaled when we pass it in (`VUID-vkAcquireNextImageKHR-semaphore-01286`) and must have no signal or wait
operations still pending on it (`VUID-vkAcquireNextImageKHR-semaphore-01779`). The fence wait above guarantees both:
the previous batch, the only operation that waited on `image_available`, has completed.

Two special results: `SUBOPTIMAL` (the image still works, it just no longer fits the surface perfectly) and
`ERROR_OUT_OF_DATE_KHR` (the swapchain no longer matches the window, for example after minimizing). Recreating the
swapchain is module 7; step 09 prints a message and stops on `OUT_OF_DATE`. When the present returns `SUBOPTIMAL`, the
program simply keeps rendering: that is allowed, the swapchain still works, it is only no longer the best fit, and
recreating it is again module 7.

## Image layouts

An [image layout](term:image-layout) is the arrangement of an image's memory for one kind of use. A GPU may keep
render targets compressed or tiled in a way that suits its render outputs, while the presentation engine needs a form it
can read. Instead of guessing, Vulkan makes us say which use comes next:

- `UNDEFINED` - "I do not care about the contents". As the *old* layout of a transition it allows the GPU to discard
  them;
- `COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OPTIMAL` - for rendering into;
- `PRESENT_SRC_KHR` - for presenting. An image must be in it when it is presented
  (`VUID-VkPresentInfoKHR-pImageIndices-01430`).

A layout changes through a **barrier** (`vkCmdPipelineBarrier2`, synchronization2), which is also an ordering rule: the
transition happens after the work in the *source* stages and before the work in the *destination* stages. This is a
preview; barriers and layouts get a full lesson in module 7.

### Two layout transitions

<Step file="09_triangle" region="barrier-to-attachment" />

`UNDEFINED → COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OPTIMAL`, before the rendering. The old contents may be discarded: we clear the whole
image anyway. The source stage is `COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OUTPUT`, the same stage at which the submit waits on
`image_available`. That chains the two: the semaphore wait blocks that stage, the barrier comes after it, so the
transition cannot start before the image is ours.

Then comes the rendering itself: `vkCmdBeginRendering` with a clear, exactly as in the
[dynamic rendering lesson](lesson:dynamic-rendering#2-begin-a-rendering), and the draw:

<Step file="09_triangle" region="draw" />

Bind the pipeline, fill in its two dynamic states (the whole image as viewport and scissor), draw **3 vertices, 1
instance**: the vertex shader runs for vertex indices 0, 1 and 2, and picks its corner from them. End the rendering.

<Step file="09_triangle" region="barrier-to-present" />

`COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OPTIMAL → PRESENT_SRC_KHR`, after the color writes. The source access `COLOR_ATTACHMENT_WRITE` says
which writes must be finished and made available; the destination stage is again `COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OUTPUT`, the stage at
which `render_finished` is signaled, so the transition is ordered before the signal. No destination access: the
presentation engine makes the writes visible to itself. Then `vkEndCommandBuffer` closes the recording.

## 4. Submit and present

The submit was shown in [the command buffers lesson](lesson:command-buffers#5-submit): wait on `image_available` at
`COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OUTPUT`, execute the buffer, signal `render_finished` for this image, and signal the fence when
everything is done. Then:

<Step file="09_triangle" region="present" />

`vkQueuePresentKHR` is a queue operation too: it is queued behind our batch and waits on `render_finished` on the
GPU side, so the CPU does not wait at all. When the semaphore is signaled the image goes back to the presentation
engine, which shows it at the next vblank (MAILBOX, chosen in the [swapchain lesson](lesson:swapchain#4-pick-a-present-mode)).

## 5. The loop and the end

<Step file="09_triangle" region="event-loop" />

winit asks for a redraw only on request, so each frame requests the next one; GLFW polls events and draws in a plain
loop. Neither has a timer: the fence wait and the present mode pace the loop.

<Step file="09_triangle" region="cleanup" />

When the window closes, the last frame may still be rendering. `vkDeviceWaitIdle` waits until all work submitted to the
device's queues has completed, and only then are the objects destroyed, newest first; the command pool frees its
command buffer. It does not wait for the presentation engine to finish showing the last image, and it does not have
to: destroying the swapchain requires only that the GPU's uses of its images have completed
(`VUID-vkDestroySwapchainKHR-swapchain-01282`). Knowing exactly when a present has released its `render_finished`
semaphore takes present fences (`VK_KHR_swapchain_maintenance1`), a topic of module 7.

The wait must also happen when something fails mid-frame. In Rust, `destroy` runs after the event loop ends, normally
or on an error. In C++ a small guard object declared just before the loop also waits, in its destructor: it runs on
every way out of `main`, including an exception, before RAII destroys the objects the GPU may still be using.

The colors are worth a second look, because they show the `_SRGB` format at work. Measured in this screenshot:

- the background is `39, 39, 48`: the linear clear color `0.02, 0.02, 0.03` encoded to sRGB;
- at the triangle's center (pixel 400, 350) the three corner colors mix in equal thirds. The shader outputs linear
  `0.333` per channel, and the pixel holds `156, 157, 156`. Stored without the sRGB curve, a third would be `85`, a
  much darker gray.

The corners are pure red, green and blue, which look the same with and without the curve; only the mixtures reveal it.

## Underneath

- The submitted command buffer is read by the command processor. When it reaches the semaphore wait it holds back the
  work of the later stages; the barrier becomes whatever the hardware needs for that transition (sometimes nothing,
  sometimes a cache flush or a change of the image's compression state); `vkCmdDraw` launches three vertex shader
  invocations, the rasterizer finds about 60 000 covered pixels, and the fragment shader runs once for each.
- The present is the hand-over to the OS: on Windows the Desktop Window Manager composes our window's image with the
  desktop. Our frame is now part of what the display scans out.
- With one frame in flight the GPU idles while the CPU records, and the CPU idles while the GPU renders. For a triangle
  it does not matter; for a real scene it does, and that is where module 7 begins.
