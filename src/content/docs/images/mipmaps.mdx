Step 23 lays step 21's sampling onto a checkerboard floor that runs to the horizon. Near the camera a texel covers many
pixels and filtering looks smooth. Far away it is the other way round: dozens of texels fall into one pixel, the
sampler reads a few of them, which ones depends on the exact position, and as the camera moves the floor dissolves into
crawling moiré. How does a renderer read "the average of all the texels under this pixel" without reading all of them,
and who prepares the data that makes it possible?

## Where this fits

The third lesson of the **images** module. It extends the [texture](lesson:textures-and-samplers) to an image with many
levels, generated with blits ordered by per-level [barriers](lesson:barriers-and-layouts), and gives the sampler's LOD
settings, which step 21 clamped to zero, real work. The chain:

`VkImage with mipLevels = 10 → copy level 0 → vkCmdBlitImage2 level i-1 → level i (one barrier dance per level) → VkImageView over all levels → VkSampler (mipmapMode, minLod, maxLod, anisotropy) → λ from the UV derivatives → texture unit reads two levels`

A [mip map](term:mipmap) is a chain of prefiltered copies of an image, each half the size of the previous one. The
sampler's [level of detail](term:lod) λ picks the level whose texels match the pixel size; blending two neighboring
levels is [trilinear filtering](term:trilinear-filtering), and [anisotropic filtering](term:anisotropic-filtering) goes
further for surfaces at grazing angles. The levels are made here by [vkCmdBlitImage2](term:vk-cmd-blit-image). Without
them, minified textures [alias](term:texture-aliasing). After this lesson: [MSAA](lesson:msaa), which treats the
aliasing at geometry edges that mipmaps cannot reach.

## Why a far floor shimmers

Sampling is measuring a signal at points. A checkerboard with 16-texel cells, seen so far away that a cell is smaller
than a pixel, contains detail finer than the pixel grid can represent; sampling it at one point per pixel folds that
detail into false low-frequency patterns, which move as the camera moves. That is [aliasing](term:texture-aliasing),
the same thing a renderer fights with pixel filters and the reason a compositor prefilters before scaling an image
down.

The cure is to remove the fine detail *before* sampling, at the right scale for each pixel. Doing that per pixel at
render time would mean averaging dozens of texels for every far pixel. Mipmaps precompute it: level 1 is level 0
averaged 2 x 2, level 2 is level 1 averaged 2 x 2, down to 1 x 1. At render time the sampler picks the level whose
texels are about one pixel in size and reads 4 texels there (8 with trilinear filtering) instead of dozens. Offline
renderers rely on the same structure: the `.tx` files that `maketx` makes for Arnold and OSL, or RenderMan's `txmake`
textures, are tiled images with a precomputed mip pyramid, so the renderer reads only the level and the tiles it needs.

## 1. How many levels, how much memory

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="mip-count" />

Each level halves width and height, rounding down, never below 1; the chain ends at 1 x 1. For a square of side n that
is floor(log2 n) + 1 levels, for a rectangle the larger side decides. For a 512 x 512 image, 10 levels:

```text
Mip levels: floor(log2(max(512, 512))) + 1 = 10
VkImage: 10 mip levels, TRANSFER_SRC | TRANSFER_DST | SAMPLED; memory 1399808 bytes (level 0 alone: 1048576; the chain adds about a third), memory type 1
```

The levels form a geometric series, 1 + 1/4 + 1/16 + ... which approaches 4/3: a full chain costs a third more than the
base image. The texels of the ten levels add up to 1398100 bytes; the driver asks for 1399808, a little more, presumably
padding of the smallest levels to its tile sizes (the layout is the driver's, so the exact reason is not visible).

## 2. Can the GPU build them?

The levels can be computed on the CPU, by an offline tool, or on the GPU. Step 23 does the last, with blits: a blit
copies a rectangle of one image into a rectangle of another, scaling and filtering on the way. That needs more from the
format than step 21 asked:

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="format-support" />

```text
Format support (vkGetPhysicalDeviceFormatProperties2):
  R8G8B8A8_SRGB (optimal tiling): SAMPLED_IMAGE yes, SAMPLED_IMAGE_FILTER_LINEAR yes, TRANSFER_DST yes, BLIT_SRC yes, BLIT_DST yes
```

`BLIT_SRC` and `BLIT_DST` let a blit read and write the format; the blit's `LINEAR` filter needs
`SAMPLED_IMAGE_FILTER_LINEAR` on the source (`VUID-VkBlitImageInfo2-filter-02001`). Formats without them get their
levels another way. Block-compressed formats typically cannot be blit *targets*: on the course GPU, `BC7_SRGB_BLOCK`
lists `BLIT_SRC` but not `BLIT_DST` (vulkaninfo `--show-formats`), since writing would mean encoding blocks. Integer
formats have no linear filtering (none does on the course GPU). Their chains come from the CPU, an offline tool or a
compute shader. Two more restrictions: blits need a queue with graphics capability
(`VUID-vkCmdBlitImage2-commandBuffer-cmdpool`), so a dedicated transfer queue cannot build mips, and neither image may
be multisampled (`VUID-VkBlitImageInfo2-srcImage-00233`, `VUID-VkBlitImageInfo2-dstImage-00234`): multisampled images
are averaged by a *resolve* instead, in the
[MSAA lesson](lesson:msaa).

## 3. The image

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="create-image" />

Two changes from step 21: `mipLevels = 10`, and the usage gains `TRANSFER_SRC`, because every level except the last is
read by a blit.

## 4. Level 0

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="upload-level-0" />

All ten levels go from `UNDEFINED` to `TRANSFER_DST_OPTIMAL` at once: level 0 for the copy, the others for the blits that
will write them. Each [subresource](lesson:image-views) (here: each mip level) has its own layout, and a barrier names
exactly which levels it moves; that is what makes the level-by-level dance below possible. Then step 21's copy fills
level 0 from the staging buffer.

## 5. The blit chain

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="blit-chain" />

Level i is made from level i-1, so level i-1 must be finished and readable first. Per level, three commands:

| Command | Level | Layout | Synchronization |
|---|---|---|---|
| barrier | i-1 | `TRANSFER_DST -> TRANSFER_SRC` | after its writer (the copy for level 0, the previous blit otherwise), before the blit's read |
| `vkCmdBlitImage2` | i-1 → i | reads `TRANSFER_SRC`, writes `TRANSFER_DST` | `LINEAR` filter, half the size |
| barrier | i-1 | `TRANSFER_SRC -> SHADER_READ_ONLY` | after the blit's read (`BLIT`, access `NONE`), before fragment shader sampling |

After the loop the last level, written but never read by a blit, gets its own `TRANSFER_DST -> SHADER_READ_ONLY`
barrier. Two details in the table are worth reading twice:

- **Access `NONE` after a read.** The third barrier orders a *read* (the blit's) before a layout transition, which is
  a write: a write-after-read. A read leaves nothing in caches that must be written back, so the execution dependency
  (the `BLIT` stage) is enough and the source access is `NONE`. Writes are different: after a write, the access must be
  named so the data is made available.
- **Linear light.** The levels must be averages of *light*, not of encoded sRGB numbers, or they would come out too
  dark. Blits handle it: "For sRGB source formats, nonlinear RGB values are converted to linear representation prior to
  filtering" (spec, "Image Copies With Scaling"); the result is converted back when written to the `_SRGB` level.

The command buffer records 30 commands for 10 levels and runs once; the program prints the chain as it records it:

```text
Recording the upload and the mip chain:
  mip  0: 512x512 (vkCmdCopyBufferToImage2 from the staging buffer)
  mip  1: 256x256 (vkCmdBlitImage2 from mip 0, LINEAR)
  mip  2: 128x128 (vkCmdBlitImage2 from mip 1, LINEAR)
  ...
  mip  9: 1x1 (vkCmdBlitImage2 from mip 8, LINEAR)
Upload + mip generation complete (fence waited); every level is SHADER_READ_ONLY_OPTIMAL
[timing] upload + 9 blits 2.392 ms
```

The upload plus nine blits took 2.4 to 3.4 ms in the runs quoted here, about what step 21's single copy took: the cost is
the submit and the fence wait, not the blits. A blit's `LINEAR` filter on an exact 2:1 reduction averages 2 x 2 texels,
a box filter. That is adequate, not the best: offline tools usually offer better kernels (Lanczos, Kaiser), which keep
distant textures sharper, one reason to precompute mips for shipped assets.

## 6. A view over all levels

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="image-view" />

The sampler can only reach levels the view exposes: `levelCount = 10`. A view with `levelCount = 1` would make the
chain invisible to shaders even though the image has it.

## 7. The sampler picks the level

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="sampler" />

The [level of detail](term:lod) λ is computed per pixel from how fast the texture coordinates change between
neighboring pixels: the implicit derivatives over a 2 x 2 quad of fragment shader invocations, scaled to texels. In the
spec's terms (without anisotropy), `λ_base = log2(ρ_max)`, where ρ_max is the larger of the two scale factors (texels
per pixel along x and y); then `λ' = λ_base + clamp(mipLodBias + shader bias, -maxSamplerLodBias, maxSamplerLodBias)`
(the shader bias is the optional `Bias` operand of the sample instruction, 0 here), and λ is λ' clamped to
`[minLod, maxLod]`. λ = 0 means one texel per pixel, λ = 3 means 8 texels per pixel, so level 3.

Then the level is picked from λ, limited to the levels of the view:

- `mipmapMode = NEAREST`: the nearest level, bilinear inside it. Visible seams where the level switches.
- `mipmapMode = LINEAR`: the two neighboring levels, bilinear in each, blended by the fraction of λ:
  [trilinear filtering](term:trilinear-filtering), which step 23 uses.

`maxLod = 10` lets λ reach the last level (anything at or above the last level index would do; `VK_LOD_CLAMP_NONE`
means "no clamp"). And λ ≤ 0 still means magnification, `magFilter`, exactly as in step 21.

### Anisotropic filtering

A floor at a grazing angle is squeezed in one direction only: a pixel's footprint in the texture is long along the view
direction and narrow across it. λ follows the *larger* scale factor, so trilinear filtering picks a level that is right
along the long axis and too blurry across it: the far floor turns into gray mush. [Anisotropic filtering](term:anisotropic-filtering)
uses a finer level and takes several samples along the long axis instead, up to `maxAnisotropy` (16 here). The spec
defines the inputs but leaves the scheme to the hardware ("implementation-dependent").

## Three runs

<Step file="23_mipmaps" region="cli" />

```text
default          Sampler: LINEAR filters, mipmapMode LINEAR, minLod 0, maxLod 10, anisotropy off (maxSamplerAnisotropy 16)
--no-mips        Sampler: LINEAR filters, mipmapMode LINEAR, minLod 0, maxLod 0 (--no-mips: level 0 only), anisotropy off (maxSamplerAnisotropy 16)
--anisotropy     Sampler: LINEAR filters, mipmapMode LINEAR, minLod 0, maxLod 10, anisotropy 16 (maxSamplerAnisotropy 16)
```

(Run names on the left added for this table; Rust and C++ print the same lines.)

- **`--no-mips`** keeps the image and its ten levels and changes one sampler field: `maxLod = 0`. λ can no longer
  exceed 0, so only level 0 is ever read, and, as in step 21, the texture always counts as magnified and `magFilter`
  is used. Expect the far floor to shimmer when the camera moves: this is the aliasing mipmaps exist to prevent.
- **default** (trilinear): the far floor should settle into a steady average, the checkerboard fading toward gray in
  the distance, which is what the prefiltered levels contain.
- **`--anisotropy`**: the same, with the cells staying distinct further toward the horizon, because finer levels are
  read along the squeezed axis.

(These are the expected results of the sampling rules; no screenshots of step 23 were taken for this lesson.)

What it costs on the course GPU: nothing measurable in this scene. Three alternating 3-second runs of each (Rust,
release, validation on): default 2646-2881 fps, `--no-mips` 2773-2847, `--anisotropy` 2722-2817. One floor quad at
800 x 600 leaves the texture units mostly idle; a frame full of anisotropically filtered textures is where the extra
samples show up in profiles.

## Generated or prepared?

Step 23 generates the chain at load time because it keeps the example self-contained. Shipped assets usually carry
their mips: KTX2 and DDS store all levels (often in block-compressed formats, which typically cannot be blit targets anyway), so
loading is one copy region per level, and the filtering is done once, offline, with a better kernel. Generating on the
GPU remains the tool for images made at run time: render targets that are sampled later, procedural or streamed
textures.

## Underneath

- **Derivatives come from quads.** Fragment shaders typically run in 2 x 2 pixel quads so that the hardware can take
  differences between neighbors; pixels outside the triangle still run as *helper invocations* to supply them. That is
  why `.Sample()` with implicit LOD is a fragment shader operation by default: compute shaders get it only through
  `VK_KHR_compute_shader_derivatives`, which groups their invocations into quads (mesh and task shaders too, where the
  device reports `meshAndTaskShaderDerivatives`), and otherwise pass the LOD or gradients explicitly. It is also why "the derivative and LOD values are undefined" (spec) when the image or sampler
  of an implicit-LOD instruction is not uniform across a quad, unless the device reports `quadDivergentImplicitLod`.
- **Texture units and caches.** Trilinear filtering reads 8 texels from two levels; because the chosen level has
  texels about the size of a pixel, neighboring pixels read neighboring texels, and the texture cache typically hits.
  Without mips, far pixels jump across level 0 and each fetch tends to touch a new cache line: mipmaps make sampling
  both better looking and kinder to the memory system.
- **Mip tails.** The smallest levels are tiny; drivers typically pack them together into one tile-sized block, which
  is consistent with the image asking for slightly more memory than its texels.
